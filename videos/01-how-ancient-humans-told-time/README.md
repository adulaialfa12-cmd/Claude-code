## 3. Script (narration)

> Fact labels for your review (remove before recording): **[F]** well-established fact ·
> **[D]** debated interpretation — the script says so out loud.

### 0:00 — Hook: A world with no time

Right now, you know roughly what time it is. Probably to the minute. Your phone knows, your laptop knows,
your microwave knows. And somewhere today, a number on a screen will decide when you eat, when you leave,
and when you're late.

Now imagine a world where not one person on Earth knows what time it is. Not because they lost their watch —
because the *hour* hasn't been invented yet. No minutes. No seconds. No "half past." No "running late."

That was the human condition for almost our entire existence. So how did people organise a day — or a year —
with no clock at all? And how did we go from that world to one where a clock decides when you work?

The answer starts somewhere you'd never think to look: inside your own body.

### 0:45 — The clock inside your body

Long before anyone carved a calendar, you were already carrying a clock. **[F]** Almost every cell in your
body runs on a cycle of roughly 24 hours — scientists call it the circadian rhythm. A small cluster of cells
behind your eyes reads the light and keeps the whole system in time with the sun.

For a hunter-gatherer, that was enough. You didn't need to know it was 6:14 a.m. You needed to know that the
sky was getting light, that the birds had started, that the animals would soon move toward water.

Time wasn't a number. It was a list of events. Dawn. The heat of midday. Long shadows. Dusk. The fire.
And it was shared — everyone around you lived by the same sky.

But a day is easy. The sun does all the work. The hard problem — the one that could kill you — is the year.
When will the herds move? When will the fruit ripen? When does the cold come back? To survive, you needed
something your body couldn't give you. You needed to *count*.

### 1:55 — Notches on a bone

In the Lebombo Mountains, on the border of South Africa and Eswatini, archaeologists found a small piece of
baboon leg bone. **[F]** It's around 43,000 years old, and someone carved 29 deliberate notches into it.

Twenty-nine. That's close to the number of days from one full moon to the next.

Here's where we have to be honest. **[D]** Some researchers think the Lebombo bone was a lunar calendar —
maybe even a way to track a menstrual cycle. Others say 29 notches could simply be a tally of anything, and
the bone might have had more notches on a part that broke off. We can't ask the person who made it.

Then there's the Ishango bone, found near the source of the Nile in what is now the Democratic Republic of
Congo. **[F]** It's roughly 20,000 years old and covered in groups of carefully cut marks. **[D]** Some
scholars see a six-month lunar calendar. Others see arithmetic, or decoration. The debate is still open.

But whichever way you read them, these bones show something important. People were trying to hold time
outside their own heads — to make it into marks you could see, touch and share.

And the moon was the perfect clock to start with. It changes shape every night, and it repeats. It's no
accident that in English, "moon" and "month" come from the same old root.

### 3:10 — Monuments that catch the sun

About 10,000 years ago, in a field in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, people dug a line of twelve pits.
**[F]** When archaeologists studied the site at Warren Field, they found the pits seem to mirror the phases
of the moon — and the whole line points toward where the sun rises in midwinter. The researchers called it
the oldest calendar ever found. **[D]** As with the bones, not everyone agrees.

Why would that matter? Because a moon-based calendar slowly drifts away from the seasons. Twelve moons come
up about eleven days short of a full year. So if you only count moons, your "spring" slowly slides into
winter. Warren Field may be people correcting their moon count against the sun — resetting the calendar
every midwinter.

Thousands of years later, people built that idea in stone. **[F]** At Stonehenge, the great stones are
aligned with the sunrise at midsummer and the sunset at midwinter. On the shortest day of the year, the sun
sets right through the heart of the monument.

Think about what that took. Generations watching the horizon. Remembering where the sun rose, year after
year. Then dragging stones weighing many tonnes into place to lock that knowledge into the landscape.

When your life depends on knowing when winter turns, your calendar isn't a wall chart. It's a monument.

### 4:40 — Egypt invents the hour

So far, everything we've seen measures days, months and years. But there's still no such thing as an hour.

That changes in ancient Egypt. **[F]** Egyptian astronomers tracked groups of stars, called decans, that
rose one after another through the night. They split the night into twelve parts. They split the daylight
into twelve parts too. Twelve plus twelve: that's where your 24-hour day comes from.

But here's the strange part. **[F]** Those hours weren't equal. Daylight was always cut into twelve — so in
summer, a daytime hour was long, and in winter, it was short. Your "hour" stretched and shrank with the
seasons. For thousands of years, in many cultures, that was perfectly normal.

To measure them, Egyptians built shadow clocks — a bar that cast a shadow across a marked scale. And for the
night, or cloudy days, they used water. **[F]** One of the oldest surviving water clocks was found at the
temple of Karnak, from around 1400 BC: a stone bowl with a tiny hole near the bottom and markings inside.
As the water dripped out, the falling level told you how much of the night had passed.

Notice who needed this. Not farmers in the field. Temple priests, who had to perform rituals at exact moments.
The first precise timekeepers weren't built for work. They were built for the gods.

And the minutes? **[F]** They come from Babylon, where mathematicians counted in sixties. That's why your hour
has sixty minutes and your minute has sixty seconds — an ancient counting system you still use every day.

### 6:10 — The bell that ran the day

Jump forward to medieval Europe. Most people still live by the sun. But inside the monasteries, time is
getting stricter.

**[F]** Monks were required to pray at set points through the day and night — the canonical hours. Someone
had to wake them, even at 2 a.m. So monasteries rang bells. And the bell did something new: it made time
*public*. Everyone in earshot — monk, farmer, merchant — now heard the same hour.

**[F]** Then, in the late 1200s, European craftsmen built the first mechanical clocks — heavy iron machines,
driven by falling weights, mounted in towers. Many didn't even have faces. They simply struck a bell.
And for centuries most clocks had only an hour hand. **[F]** Clocks only became accurate enough for a
minute hand to really matter after the pendulum clock arrived in 1656.

Once a town had a clock tower, the hour belonged to the town. Markets opened by it. Gates closed by it.
For the first time, ordinary people's days were being set by a machine.

### 7:25 — The clock becomes your boss

And then came the factory.

**[D/F]** In 1967, the historian E. P. Thompson described a shift that changed everything. Before industry,
he argued, most people's work was "task-oriented." You worked until the job was done. The cows got milked
when the cows needed milking. The harvest was brought in when the weather allowed. Work and life blurred
together, and nobody asked how many hours it took.

Factories didn't work that way. Hundreds of people had to start the same machines at the same moment.
So time stopped being about the task and became about the clock. Bosses paid for hours, not for jobs. Bells
and whistles marked the start of the shift. Being late meant fines. **[F]** In Britain, some workers even
paid "knocker-uppers" — people who walked the streets before dawn tapping on bedroom windows with long poles
— so they wouldn't miss the factory bell.

Even the time itself had to be standardised. Until the 1800s, every town set its clocks by its own noon,
so clocks in neighbouring cities disagreed by minutes. Railways made that impossible — trains need one shared
timetable. **[F]** On 18 November 1883, American and Canadian railroads switched to standard time zones.
People called it "the day of two noons," because many towns' clocks had to jump on that day.

In less than two centuries, humanity went from "work until the job is done" to "work from nine to five."

### 9:05 — What we lost

In the 1930s, the anthropologist E. E. Evans-Pritchard lived with the Nuer people of South Sudan.
**[F]** He noticed they didn't describe the day in hours. They described it by the rhythm of the cattle:
the time of milking, the time of taking the herds to pasture, the time of their return. He called it a
"cattle clock." Time wasn't something you spent. It was something you lived inside.

That's the world almost every human being has lived in. The body woke you. The moon counted your months.
The sun told you when the year turned. The hour only appeared around 4,000 years ago — and the minute only
became part of ordinary life a few centuries ago.

So the next time a screen tells you you're late, remember: the clock is one of humanity's greatest
inventions. But it's also a very young one. And for most of our history, nobody was ever late — because
there was nothing to be late *for*.

If you want to understand how work took over our time, the next chapter of that story is in the video on
screen now. And if you enjoyed this, subscribe — every video here asks one question: *why do we work the
way we do?*
