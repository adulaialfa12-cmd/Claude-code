And then came the factory.

 In 1967, the historian E. P. Thompson described a shift that changed everything. Before industry, he argued, most people's work was "task-oriented." You worked until the job was done. The cows got milked when the cows needed milking. The harvest was brought in when the weather allowed. Work and life blurred together, and nobody asked how many hours it took.

Factories didn't work that way. Hundreds of people had to start the same machines at the same moment. So time stopped being about the task and became about the clock. Bosses paid for hours, not for jobs. Bells and whistles marked the start of the shift. Being late meant fines. In Britain, some workers even paid "knocker-uppers" — people who walked the streets before dawn tapping on bedroom windows with long poles — so they wouldn't miss the factory bell.

Even the time itself had to be standardised. Until the 1800s, every town set its clocks by its own noon, so clocks in neighbouring cities disagreed by minutes. Railways made that impossible — trains need one shared timetable. On 18 November 1883, American and Canadian railroads switched to standard time zones. People called it "the day of two noons," because many towns' clocks had to jump on that day.

In less than two centuries, humanity went from "work until the job is done" to "work from nine to five."
