About 10,000 years ago, in a field in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, people dug a line of twelve pits.  When archaeologists studied the site at Warren Field, they found the pits seem to mirror the phases of the moon — and the whole line points toward where the sun rises in midwinter. The researchers called it the oldest calendar ever found. As with the bones, not everyone agrees.

Why would that matter? Because a moon-based calendar slowly drifts away from the seasons. Twelve moons come up about eleven days short of a full year. So if you only count moons, your "spring" slowly slides into winter. Warren Field may be people correcting their moon count against the sun — resetting the calendar every midwinter.

Thousands of years later, people built that idea in stone. At Stonehenge, the great stones are aligned with the sunrise at midsummer and the sunset at midwinter. On the shortest day of the year, the sun sets right through the heart of the monument.

Think about what that took. Generations watching the horizon. Remembering where the sun rose, year after year. Then dragging stones weighing many tonnes into place to lock that knowledge into the landscape.

When your life depends on knowing when winter turns, your calendar isn't a wall chart. It's a monument.
