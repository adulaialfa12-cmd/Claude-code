In the Lebombo Mountains, on the border of South Africa and Eswatini, archaeologists found a small piece of baboon leg bone. It's around 43,000 years old, and someone carved 29 deliberate notches into it.

Twenty-nine. That's close to the number of days from one full moon to the next.

Here's where we have to be honest. Some researchers think the Lebombo bone was a lunar calendar — maybe even a way to track a menstrual cycle. Others say 29 notches could simply be a tally of anything, and the bone might have had more notches on a part that broke off. We can't ask the person who made it.

Then there's the Ishango bone, found near the source of the Nile in what is now the Democratic Republic of Congo. It's roughly 20,000 years old and covered in groups of carefully cut marks. Some scholars see a six-month lunar calendar. Others see arithmetic, or decoration. The debate is still open.

But whichever way you read them, these bones show something important. People were trying to hold time outside their own heads — to make it into marks you could see, touch and share.

And the moon was the perfect clock to start with. It changes shape every night, and it repeats. It's no accident that in English, "moon" and "month" come from the same old root.
