So far, everything we've seen measures days, months and years. But there's still no such thing as an hour.

That changes in ancient Egypt. Egyptian astronomers tracked groups of stars, called decans, that rose one after another through the night. They split the night into twelve parts. They split the daylight into twelve parts too. Twelve plus twelve: that's where your 24-hour day comes from.

But here's the strange part. Those hours weren't equal. Daylight was always cut into twelve — so in summer, a daytime hour was long, and in winter, it was short. Your "hour" stretched and shrank with the seasons. For thousands of years, in many cultures, that was perfectly normal.

To measure them, Egyptians built shadow clocks — a bar that cast a shadow across a marked scale. And for the night, or cloudy days, they used water. One of the oldest surviving water clocks was found at the temple of Karnak, from around 1400 BC: a stone bowl with a tiny hole near the bottom and markings inside. As the water dripped out, the falling level told you how much of the night had passed.

Notice who needed this. Not farmers in the field. Temple priests, who had to perform rituals at exact moments. The first precise timekeepers weren't built for work. They were built for the gods.

And the minutes? They come from Babylon, where mathematicians counted in sixties. That's why your hour has sixty minutes and your minute has sixty seconds — an ancient counting system you still use every day.
