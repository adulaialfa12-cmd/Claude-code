Long before anyone carved a calendar, you were already carrying a clock. Almost every cell in your body runs on a cycle of roughly 24 hours — scientists call it the circadian rhythm. A small cluster of cells behind your eyes reads the light and keeps the whole system in time with the sun.

For a hunter-gatherer, that was enough. You didn't need to know it was 6:14 a.m. You needed to know that the sky was getting light, that the birds had started, that the animals would soon move toward water.

Time wasn't a number. It was a list of events. Dawn. The heat of midday. Long shadows. Dusk. The fire. And it was shared — everyone around you lived by the same sky.

But a day is easy. The sun does all the work. The hard problem — the one that could kill you — is the year. When will the herds move? When will the fruit ripen? When does the cold come back? To survive, you needed something your body couldn't give you. You needed to count.
