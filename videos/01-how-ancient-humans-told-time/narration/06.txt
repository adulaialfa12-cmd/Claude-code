Jump forward to medieval Europe. Most people still live by the sun. But inside the monasteries, time is getting stricter.

 Monks were required to pray at set points through the day and night — the canonical hours. Someone had to wake them, even at 2 a.m. So monasteries rang bells. And the bell did something new: it made time public. Everyone in earshot — monk, farmer, merchant — now heard the same hour.

 Then, in the late 1200s, European craftsmen built the first mechanical clocks — heavy iron machines, driven by falling weights, mounted in towers. Many didn't even have faces. They simply struck a bell. And for centuries most clocks had only an hour hand. Clocks only became accurate enough for a minute hand to really matter after the pendulum clock arrived in 1656.

Once a town had a clock tower, the hour belonged to the town. Markets opened by it. Gates closed by it. For the first time, ordinary people's days were being set by a machine.
