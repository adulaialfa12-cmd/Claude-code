In the 1930s, the anthropologist E. E. Evans-Pritchard lived with the Nuer people of South Sudan.  He noticed they didn't describe the day in hours. They described it by the rhythm of the cattle: the time of milking, the time of taking the herds to pasture, the time of their return. He called it a "cattle clock." Time wasn't something you spent. It was something you lived inside.

That's the world almost every human being has lived in. The body woke you. The moon counted your months. The sun told you when the year turned. The hour only appeared around 4,000 years ago — and the minute only became part of ordinary life a few centuries ago.

So the next time a screen tells you you're late, remember: the clock is one of humanity's greatest inventions. But it's also a very young one. And for most of our history, nobody was ever late — because there was nothing to be late for.
