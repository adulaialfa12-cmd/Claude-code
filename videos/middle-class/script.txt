For almost all of human history, there was no middle class. Not a small one. Not a struggling one. In most societies, almost none at all. A tiny group at the top owned the land, and nearly everyone else worked it and lived close to the edge. Then, in the middle of the twentieth century, something strange happened. In just a few decades, tens of millions of ordinary working families got houses, cars, pensions, and college degrees. And here is the uncomfortable part. Many historians think this did not happen because the system was designed to produce it. It happened because the world had just been through a catastrophe.

So in this video, we are going to follow the middle class from ancient Rome to a medieval plague, to a car factory in Detroit, to the world's deadliest war, and finally to your own paycheck. By the end, you will understand why the comfortable middle life many of us grew up expecting may have been the exception, not the rule. And why knowing that can actually help you make calmer, smarter decisions about your own work and money today.

Let's start two thousand years ago.

Imagine you are living in the Roman Empire around the year one hundred and fifty. It is one of the richest societies the ancient world ever built. Surely a place this wealthy had a big comfortable middle.

But that is not what the evidence suggests. The Romans never kept national income statistics, so modern historians have to reconstruct the picture from scattered clues. One well known estimate, by the historians Walter Scheidel and Steven Friesen, suggests that a tiny elite, around one and a half percent of the population, took roughly a fifth of all income. A middling group, people who lived comfortably above subsistence, may have been somewhere between six and twelve percent of people. Everyone else, the great majority, lived at or near the level of bare survival.

The authors call this controlled conjecture. The exact numbers are uncertain, but the shape is clear. A thin layer on top, a narrow band in the middle, and a huge base at the bottom.

The ancient world already knew a big middle was valuable. Centuries earlier, the Greek philosopher Aristotle wrote in his Politics that a state with a large middle group of citizens tends to be the most stable, because it is least likely to be torn apart by fights between the rich and the poor. But he added a gloomy observation. In most cities, he said, the middle was small. So power usually swung toward either the wealthy few or the many poor.

People understood the value of a middle class two thousand years ago. They just almost never had one.

So what would it actually take to change that? The first real clue came from an unexpected place. Not from a law or a revolution, but from a disease.

In the late 1340s, the Black Death swept across Europe. Historians estimate it killed somewhere between a third and a half of the population in many regions.

But it had a strange economic side effect. The fields and the landlords were still there. The hands needed to work them were gone. And when workers become scarce, they become valuable.

Laborers began asking for more money. The English government panicked. In 1351 it passed a law called the Statute of Labourers, which tried to force workers to accept the wages they had earned before the plague and banned employers from paying more.

It did not work very well. The law was hard to enforce and deeply resented, and that anger is often linked to the great Peasants' Revolt of 1381.

What happened to ordinary living standards afterward is still debated. Some economic historians see a golden age for laborers in the following century. Others argue the gains were smaller or slower. But most agree on the pattern. When population recovered, the advantage faded, and the old hierarchy returned.

Keep that pattern in mind. A shock makes labor scarce. Workers gain power. Then things drift back. We will see it again, on a much bigger scale.

Fast forward to the early nineteen hundreds. The industrial revolution has made the world richer than ever before. You might expect that wealth to be spread widely.

It was not. Economists Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, studying old tax records, estimate that just before the First World War, the richest ten percent of people in countries like Britain, France, and Germany received roughly forty five to fifty percent of all income. In the United States, it was a little above forty percent. Europeans later called this period the Belle Epoque, the beautiful era. It was beautiful mostly for the people at the top.

But even in this world, there were hints of what was coming. In January 1914, Henry Ford announced something that shocked the business world. He would pay many of his factory workers five dollars for an eight hour day. Before that, the rate was about two dollars and thirty four cents for a nine hour day. Roughly double.

Why? Not mainly out of kindness. Ford had a problem. The work on his new assembly lines was so repetitive and exhausting that people kept quitting. According to the Henry Ford museum, his turnover in 1913 was close to three hundred and eighty percent. Paying more was cheaper than constantly replacing workers.

There was a catch. To qualify, workers had their private lives checked by company investigators.

Still, Ford had stumbled onto an idea that would shape the coming century. But for now, it was the exception. To turn it into the rule, the world would need something much bigger. And much darker.

In 1939 the Second World War began, and it changed the economy of almost every country it touched.

Governments needed enormous amounts of production. Millions of men left for the front, so factories hired women and workers who had been shut out before. Labor was scarce again, just like after the plague. But this time the government was directly involved.

In the United States, a body called the National War Labor Board oversaw wages during the war. Top tax rates rose to very high levels. And something remarkable happened to pay.

Economists Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo called it the Great Compression. Studying wage data, they found that the gap between high paid and low paid men in America narrowed sharply during the 1940s. Most of that narrowing happened in a single decade. They give some credit to the war and the wage board, but they also point to a strong demand for less skilled workers at a time when education was expanding fast.

In Europe the change was even more dramatic. The share of income going to the top ten percent fell from that forty five to fifty percent range down to around thirty percent by the 1950s and 1960s.

Now here is the part that gives this video its title. The Stanford historian Walter Scheidel studied inequality across thousands of years. In his book The Great Leveler, he argues that big reductions in inequality have almost always come from four violent forces. He calls them the Four Horsemen. Mass mobilization warfare, revolutions that transform society, state collapse, and deadly pandemics.

That claim is controversial. For example, one study of Finland found that inequality fell significantly in the late eighteen hundreds through broad economic growth, without a great catastrophe. So it is fair to say this is debated. But the mid twentieth century fits Scheidel's pattern uncomfortably well.

And yet, a war alone does not build suburbs. What came next was a set of choices.

In 1944, before the war had even ended, the United States passed the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, better known as the G I Bill. It offered returning veterans help with education, job training, and home loans.

According to figures cited by the National Park Service, about seven point eight million World War Two veterans used its education and training benefits by 1956. Many went to college. Many more learned a trade. The program also guaranteed around two point four million home loans between 1944 and 1952.

In 1940, about forty four percent of American households owned their home. By 1960, it was about sixty two percent. A Federal Reserve note describes this as the biggest change in American homeownership in the past century.

But it is important to be honest about who was left out. The benefits were run through local banks, colleges, and officials, and in practice many Black veterans were blocked from using them fully, especially for housing. The new middle class was real, but it was not open to everyone.

At the same time, unions were at their peak. In the mid nineteen fifties, roughly one in three American workers belonged to a union. In 1950, the United Auto Workers and General Motors signed a contract that the business press nicknamed the Treaty of Detroit. It tied raises to inflation and to rising productivity, and it included pensions and health benefits. A factory worker could now plan a whole life. A house. A car. A retirement.

To the people living through it, this felt like the normal reward for hard work. Like this was simply how a modern economy works.

But was it? Or was it a moment, made possible by a world war, a labor shortage, unusual government policy, and an American economy that faced little competition from countries rebuilding after the war? That question is exactly what the next fifty years would answer.

Starting around the 1970s, the pattern began to reverse. Goldin and Margo found that the American wage gap, which had narrowed so sharply in the 1940s, widened greatly after 1970. By the late 1980s, by their measure, it was back to about where it had been in 1940. Piketty and Saez found that the share of income going to the top ten percent in the United States climbed back toward the levels of a century earlier.

Union membership fell steadily. By 2025, according to government data, it was around ten percent of employed workers.

And the middle itself got thinner. The Pew Research Center found that in 1971, sixty one percent of Americans lived in middle income households. By 2023, it was fifty one percent.

But this is where we need to be careful, because the headline can mislead. Over that same period, Pew found the share of Americans in lower income households rose from twenty seven to thirty percent. The share in upper income households rose from eleven to nineteen percent. So the middle shrank in both directions. Some people slipped down. But more actually moved up. The story is not simply that everyone got poorer. It is that the middle stopped being the place where almost everyone ended up.

Why did this happen? Economists still argue about it. Common explanations include global trade, technology that rewards some skills far more than others, the decline of unions, and changes in tax policy. Most likely, it is several of these together.

So what does all of this mean for you?

First, if you have ever felt that you work as hard as your parents did, yet the same milestones feel harder to reach, you are not imagining things. The mid twentieth century was unusual. Measuring yourself against it is like measuring every summer against the hottest one on record.

Second, history shows that what made the middle class grow was not just effort. It was bargaining power. After the plague, workers had power because they were scarce. Ford's workers had power because he could not keep them. Postwar workers had power through skills, unions, and a booming economy. Today, the same basic idea still applies at a personal level. Skills that are hard to replace, knowledge of what your work is worth, and more than one possible source of income all tend to give people more choices. That is not financial advice. It is simply the lesson that runs through the whole story.

Third, it is a reminder that the middle class was partly an accident, but also partly a choice. The war was a catastrophe no one would want to repeat. But the G I Bill, mass education, and the rules around work and pay were decisions people made. Historians and economists still debate whether broad prosperity can be built again without a disaster. Scheidel himself is doubtful. Others point to examples of more inclusive growth. That debate is still open, and it may be one of the most important economic questions of our time.

So let's bring it all together.

For most of history, there was no large middle. The Black Death gave workers a brief window of power. The industrial age created enormous wealth but kept most of it at the top. Then a world war, a labor shortage, and a set of bold policies created something new. A broad, comfortable middle that, for a while, felt permanent.

It was not permanent. But understanding where it came from helps you see your own situation not as a personal failure, and not as fate, but as part of a much longer story about work, power, and who gets to share in a society's wealth.
