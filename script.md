## Narration

### Hook

Our species is about three hundred thousand years old. Money, even if we're generous, is about five thousand. That means for more than ninety-eight percent of human history, nobody had any money at all. No coins. No wages. No prices. And yet, every single morning, people woke up and filled their day with something.

So what were they doing? By the end of this video, you'll know how they spent their hours, how they traded without money, and what they saved up instead of cash.

### Chapter 1 — How long did we live without money?

Let's start with the timeline. The oldest known fossils of our species come from a site in Morocco called Jebel Irhoud. They're about three hundred thousand years old. Now let's look for money. About four thousand one hundred years ago, in Mesopotamia, a law code was already setting fines in silver. Knock out another man's tooth, and you owed two shekels of silver. But a shekel back then was a weight, not a coin. You paid with measured lumps of metal.

Real coins came much later. They appeared in Lydia, in what is now western Turkey, about two thousand six hundred years ago. They were small lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, stamped with a lion's head. Now, squeeze all of human history into one day. Silver prices show up about twenty minutes before midnight. Coins show up about twelve minutes before midnight. Everything else, the whole long day, happened without a single price tag. So how did people get what they needed? Most of us learned the answer in school. It's called barter. But is it true?

### Chapter 2 — Did they really barter?

Here's the story from the textbooks. One person has extra grain. Another has extra meat. They swap. Swapping is clumsy, so someday someone invents money to make it easier. It's a neat story. The problem is, nobody has ever found a society that actually worked this way. In 1985, the Cambridge anthropologist Caroline Humphrey looked at the evidence. She wrote that no example of a pure barter economy had ever been described. Where barter does show up, it's usually between strangers, or between people who already know what money is.

Inside a small group, people did something different. They gave. If I find honey today, I share it, and I don't ask for anything back. But everyone remembers. This kept everyone fed even when one person's luck ran out. Next week, when you bring home meat, you share with me. Anthropologists call this delayed reciprocity. It's less like a bill and more like a long, friendly memory. Exactly how money first appeared is still debated. But the picture of our ancestors haggling all day, one swap at a time, doesn't match what researchers have found. So if they weren't trading all day, what did a normal day look like?

### Chapter 3 — What did a normal day look like?

Here's the tricky part. For almost all of this time, nobody wrote anything down. So scientists use two kinds of clues. The first is what people left behind, like tools, bones, hearths and beads. The second is careful study of people who lived mostly by foraging in the last hundred years, like the Hadza in Tanzania and the Ju/'hoansi in the Kalahari. These people are not frozen in the past. They have their own long histories. But they show us what a day can look like with no money, no shops and no fields.

A day often started slowly, around the fire. A Hadza camp is small, usually around thirty people, living in simple grass huts. Then small groups headed out. Among the Hadza, women often went out together with their children to dig up tubers, pick berries and collect baobab fruit. Men more often went alone or in pairs, carrying bows. Some followed a small bird called the honeyguide. Hadza hunters whistle to it, and the bird leads them to a wild bees' nest. The people get the honey, and the bird gets the wax. Children joined in early. Researchers who followed Hadza kids found that some gathered more food in a day than an adult needs, mostly fruit near camp. So how many hours did all of this actually take?

### Chapter 4 — How many hours did they work?

In the 1960s, the anthropologist Richard Lee measured how long Ju/'hoansi adults spent getting food. His answer was about twelve to nineteen hours a week. Another anthropologist, Marshall Sahlins, used numbers like these to call foragers the original affluent society. Rich, not because they had a lot, but because they needed little and had time to spare.

That idea became famous. It's also debated. When Lee later counted the other work too, like making tools, cracking nuts and keeping camp, the total rose to about forty to forty-four hours a week. That's close to a modern full-time job. Part of the gap is about definitions: is cracking nuts work, or is it sitting with friends? So which is it? A newer study helps. In the Philippines, researchers followed three hundred and fifty-nine Agta people through the day. Agta who mostly foraged did about twenty hours of work outside camp each week. Agta who mostly farmed did about thirty.

So here's the honest answer. Foragers did work, and some of it was hard and dangerous. But it came in bursts, and it was mixed in with talking, resting and being with family. Some days were long and hard, like a hunt that went on until dark. Other days were quiet. The line between work and the rest of life was blurry. So what filled all those other hours?

### Chapter 5 — What filled the rest of the day?

A lot of it looked like what we'd call hanging out. People sat in the shade and fixed things. They straightened arrows, scraped hides, and rolled plant fibers into string. Babies were rarely alone. Among the Hadza, researchers saw infants being held by grandmothers, older siblings and neighbors, not just by their mothers. Kids played in mixed-age groups, and a lot of that play copied adult work. Tiny bows. Little digging sticks. Pretend hunts.

And people talked. A lot. The anthropologist Polly Wiessner took notes on Ju/'hoansi conversations in the 1970s. During the day, about a third of the talk was complaints and criticism, often about someone who wasn't sharing or who was acting too proud. Joking was common too, about one in six daytime conversations. In a group with no police and no boss, that talk did a job. If you were stingy, everyone would hear about it by evening. So daytime talk was about keeping the rules. But something changed when the sun went down.

### Chapter 6 — What happened by the fire at night?

We're about halfway now. We've seen how long people lived without money, why barter is mostly a myth, and how the daylight hours went. Still to come: what happened after dark, how people traded across huge distances, and what they saved instead of money.

After dark, Wiessner found, the talk around the fire changed. More than eighty percent of the night conversations she noted were stories. In daylight, stories made up only about six percent. People told stories about hunts, about relatives in faraway camps, and about the spirit world. Firelight talk, Wiessner argued, helped people keep a picture of the wider world in their heads: who was related to whom, and who lived where. There was singing and dancing too. And the complaints of the day mostly faded away.

People didn't go to sleep at sunset, either. A study of three groups, including the Hadza and the San, found that people stayed up about three hours after dark. They slept around six to seven hours a night, not much different from many of us. Fire made all of this possible. Archaeologists see regular use of fire from at least about four hundred thousand years ago, and the earliest dates are still debated. Those evening hours by the fire were when the group was bound together. But stories didn't only travel around one fire. Some things traveled a very long way. How?

### Chapter 7 — How did they trade with people far away?

At a site in Kenya called Olorgesailie, archaeologists found small, sharp tools made of obsidian, a black volcanic glass. The tools are around three hundred thousand years old. And the obsidian came from at least twenty-five to fifty kilometers away. Did people walk that far to get it? Or did it pass from hand to hand between groups? We can't be sure. But either way, people were reaching far beyond their own camp.

Among the Ju/'hoansi, we can see one way this works. They had a custom called hxaro. You give someone a gift, like a bead necklace or an arrow. Later, they give you one back. Not right away, and never exactly equal. Hxaro partners could live as far as two hundred kilometers apart. In 1974, Wiessner found that about sixty-nine percent of the things people owned had come to them as hxaro gifts.

Here's why it mattered. If the rain failed where you lived, you could go and stay with your hxaro partners for months. The gift wasn't really about the necklace. It was a promise that someone, somewhere, would take you in. Researchers have described this as a kind of insurance, spread across a whole region. So why were beads so often the thing people gave?

### Chapter 8 — Why spend hours on beads, art and music?

Beads are some of the oldest signs of this kind of behavior. In Bizmoune Cave in Morocco, archaeologists found shell beads that are at least one hundred and forty-two thousand years old, the oldest known. Some still had traces of red ochre. People at Olorgesailie were already carrying red pigment, too. Making beads takes time. For an ostrich eggshell bead, you break off a small piece of shell, drill a hole with a stone point, then string it and grind the edges round. A whole necklace means many, many hours of careful work.

In 2022, two researchers compared more than fifteen hundred ostrich eggshell beads from across eastern and southern Africa. Between about fifty thousand and thirty-three thousand years ago, beads made three thousand kilometers apart were almost the same size and shape. They argue this points to a social network stretching across that distance. Whether the beads themselves traveled, or only the know-how, is still debated.

People also made music. Flutes carved from bird bone and mammoth ivory, found in a German cave called Geissenklösterle, are around forty-two thousand years old. Beads, songs and stories don't fill your stomach. But they show who you are, and who you belong to. In a world without money, that was a kind of wealth. So what did people actually own?

### Chapter 9 — What did they own and save?

Not much. People who move camp often can only own what they can carry. A bow, a digging stick, a bag, a few tools, and some ornaments. And even what they owned was often given away again, passed along as gifts. Big food was rarely private. Among the Hadza, when a hunter brings home a large animal, he doesn't get to control who gets the meat. It's shared widely around camp. And if you have more than you need and someone asks, you're expected to give.

Why would anyone go along with this? Think about a hunter's luck. Even good hunters often come home with nothing for days. And without a fridge, a big animal spoils fast. So the safest place to keep extra meat was in your neighbors. Next time, they'd feed you. That's the answer to our opening question. Before money, people did save. They saved favors, friendships and trust. A good name was the closest thing to a bank account.

It wasn't a paradise. Everyone knew your business, and the pressure to share could feel heavy. Life was risky too. Studies of foraging groups suggest that close to half of all children died before they grew up. So if this system worked for so long, why did money take over?

### Chapter 10 — Why did money appear at all?

Things started to change when people began farming, about eleven thousand five hundred years ago in the Middle East, and later in other places. Farming meant staying put and storing food. Stored grain can be counted, guarded and owed. Villages grew into towns, and towns into cities. Soon you were dealing with people you might never see again. A favor only works if you'll meet that person again. With strangers, you need something that keeps score on its own.

Coins made that easier still. A stamped coin carries a promise about its weight, so you don't need a scale every time you pay. In Mesopotamia, some of the earliest writing we have, from about five thousand years ago, is mostly bookkeeping. Lists of grain, beer, animals and workers. Money grew up inside that world of records, debts and cities. Exactly how it happened is still debated by historians and economists.

So let's go back to our one-day clock. For almost the whole day, people woke up, looked for food, fixed their tools, held each other's babies, complained about the neighbor who didn't share, and told stories by the fire. Their wealth was each other. Money only shows up in the last few minutes. That's what ancient humans did all day before money existed. They lived inside a web of people. In a way, we still do. Money just changed how we keep score.
